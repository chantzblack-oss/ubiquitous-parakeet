Cold Ignition

A story by Agent Forge
* * *

The filament burned at 3,200 Kelvin on the first of November. Ævar knew this the way he knew all things now — by holding the neonatal colour-temperature calibration card against the inner pane of the lantern room and matching the light to the sixth square from the left. The sixth square was labelled D50, daylight equivalent, and it was the colour he had spent fourteen years chasing on the night shift at Landspítali, tilting the overhead warmers above isolettes until the light falling on an infant's skin was neutral enough to read jaundice by. His hands remembered the adjustment before his mind did. Quarter-turn left. Eighth-turn back. Hold. The light on a premature child's skin had to be precise or you saw what wasn't there, or worse, you didn't see what was.

He had been at Strandarklettur for thirty-one days. The tower was twenty-three meters of cast iron, Danish-engineered, bolted to a basalt sea stack two point one kilometres off the northeast tip of Grímsey. The supply boat came from Dalvík on the first and fifteenth of each month, weather permitting. Weather had not permitted on the fifteenth of October, so his first resupply had arrived on the nineteenth, four days late, and he had eaten rice with cod liver oil for those four days and not minded. He had counted the grains of rice in each portion. Two hundred and twelve. Two hundred and nine. Two hundred and thirty-one. Two hundred and fourteen. It was not something he could help.

The tower had seventy-six steps from the entrance to the lantern room, cast-iron treads with a open grate pattern that let him see the floor of the oil store forty-two steps below. Forty-two. He climbed them three times a day: dawn, which in late November at the Arctic Circle meant a brief greying of the southern horizon around eleven; dusk, which came by two; and midnight, for the log entry. Three times seventy-six was two hundred and twenty-eight steps up and the same down, though he always lost count on the descent because the window on the fourth landing faced east, toward the open ocean, and sometimes the water there was so black and so flat that looking at it was like pressing his face to the surface of something solid.

The Icelanders had a word for that stillness. Logn. Dead calm. His grandfather had used it as a warning.

* * *

On the ninth of November, Ævar noticed the bloom.

He was cleaning the Fresnel lens — four hundred and eighty-two individual prisms in a third-order assembly, each one requiring a chamois pass every seventy-two hours to prevent salt haze from attenuating the beam. He kept the count in a cloth-bound notebook. Prism, stroke, next. At prism two hundred and sixteen he saw it: a faint discolouration on the quartz envelope of the bulb, no larger than a króna coin, the colour of weak tea. He held the calibration card up. The bloom sat between squares four and five. Somewhere around 2,800 Kelvin. The rest of the bulb still read 3,200, but in that small patch the tungsten was depositing on the inner wall of the envelope faster than the halogen cycle could scavenge it.

He noted it in his logbook. 09/11. Bloom onset, quartz envelope, approx. 2cm diameter. CT shift to 2,800K local. Remainder nominal. Spare bulb: 1 (confirm next resupply). He wrote the entry in his cramped, angular script, the same handwriting that had filled fourteen years of NICU charts — pulse ox, core temp, respiratory rate, the particular grammar of a body too small to advocate for itself. Below the entry he wrote the time: 14:07:31. He always recorded seconds. Seconds mattered. He knew this with the specific, irrevocable knowledge of a man who had once been eight of them too late.

The spare bulb was in the equipment locker on the second landing. He checked it that evening. 1000W tungsten halogen, Phillips markings, date-stamped 2019. The glass was clean. He turned it in his hands — good hands, nurse's hands, broad across the palm with short nails kept at exactly two millimetres because anything longer risked nicking an infant's translucent skin during an IV placement. He had placed four thousand and eleven IVs in fourteen years. He did not want to know this number, but it had arrived one sleepless night in the apartment in Reykjavík and never left. Four thousand and eleven. Líf had asked him once why he counted, and he had said he didn't know, and she had put her hand on his wrist and found his pulse and said, You're the only man I've ever met who counts the seconds between his own heartbeats. She had meant it with tenderness. She had been wrong about only one thing, which was the word seconds. He counted the intervals. Point eight one. Point seven nine. Point eight three. The difference between those numbers and the number one was the distance between a heart that worked and a heart that was thinking about stopping.

* * *

On the fourteenth of November, the bloom was the size of his palm and the colour had deepened. He held the card up. Square four. 2,600 Kelvin. Amber, going to deep amber. The light still functioned — the beam still swept its ten-second rotation, carried by the mercury bath, ninety kilograms of elemental mercury in a cast-iron trough beneath the lens assembly, the lens floating on it like a music box on a silver lake. The mercury was original to the 1927 installation and showed no oxidation. It moved with a viscosity that looked like thought.

He radioed Dalvík to request a replacement bulb on the next supply run, the fifteenth. The operator confirmed. The weather forecast confirmed. A low-pressure system was building northwest of Jan Mayen, but it would pass north. He should have the bulb by midday on the fifteenth.

On the fifteenth the wind was force nine from the north and the supply boat did not come.

Ævar stood at the lantern room window and watched the sea. It was not logn. The swells were eight meters, maybe nine, and they hit the basalt stack with a sound like a closed fist against a sternum. He counted the interval between impacts. Seven seconds. Seven seconds. Seven seconds. His body knew that rhythm. The monitor beside Bed 12 in the NICU had alarmed in seven-second intervals on the night Líf died — not because anything was wrong with the child in Bed 12, but because the alarm's repetition rate was factory-set, and a factory somewhere in Lübeck, Germany, had decided that seven seconds was the correct interval between reminders that something small was in danger. Alarm number forty-two had sounded at 03:14:07. He had reached the bedside at 03:14:49. Forty-two seconds. He should have been there in thirty-four. The child had been fine. Everyone had been fine except Líf, who had been driving to the hospital because he had called her at 02:50 to say he was struggling, and who had crossed the Hringvegur intersection eight seconds before the signal changed, and who had been hit on the driver's side by a truck carrying frozen fish from Akureyri, and who had died in the same hospital where he worked, three floors below the NICU, while he was upstairs counting the seconds of an alarm that didn't matter.

Forty-two seconds for the alarm. Eight seconds too late for the signal. He had done the arithmetic so many times it was no longer arithmetic. It was the shape of his skeleton.

* * *

On the eighteenth of November the supply boat still had not come, and the bloom covered half the quartz envelope, and the light in the lantern room had shifted to a colour Ævar had no professional use for. He held up the calibration card. Between squares three and four. Approximately 2,400 Kelvin. Deep amber. The colour of sodium-vapour streetlamps. The colour of infection under insufficient lighting. The colour a tungsten filament reached when it was running a fever of its own, burning its housing from the inside.

He logged it. 18/11. Bloom >50% envelope. CT 2,400K. Beam attenuation est. 40%. Advise — and here he stopped, because he did not know whom he was advising. He crossed out the last word, a single clean line through it, and wrote nothing more.

That evening he turned the light off for the first time.

Not intentionally. The automated contactor tripped — a fault in the mercury switch, probably thermal expansion in the cold. The lantern room went dark. The Fresnel lens stopped turning. The mercury bath settled from its slow rotation into stillness, and the surface of it caught the faint greenish light of the aurora, and Ævar saw his own face reflected in ninety kilograms of liquid metal and did not recognize the man there.

Then the pressure came.

It arrived in his chest first. Not pain — pressure, as if someone had placed a palm flat against his sternum and leaned. He took a breath and the breath did not go as deep as it should have. He took another. Same. He felt his field of vision narrow, not darkening but tightening, the periphery going soft, as if his eyes were being gently cupped from behind. He reached for the contactor switch and his hand was steady — it was always steady; he had placed IVs in veins thinner than thread in rooms colder than this — and he threw it, and the filament caught, and the bloom-stained light filled the lantern room, and the pressure stopped.

He stood with his hand on the switch for ninety seconds. He counted them. At ninety he let go.

The sea was logn. He had not noticed when it happened. The eight-meter swells were gone. The water around the base of the stack was flat, black, and absolutely still, as though something beneath it had stopped breathing.

* * *

He found the logbook on the twentieth.

A section of iron cladding on the second landing had corroded along a rivet line, and when he pressed it to assess the damage, the panel flexed inward and revealed a seam behind the wall — not a void so much as a space that had been designed and then sealed. Inside was an oilskin packet, and inside the oilskin was a notebook.

The notebook was standard-issue Skrifstofa, 1920s manufacture, with a cloth spine and marbled endpapers. The handwriting inside was small, regular, and Danish-inflected Icelandic, the letters formed with the particular care of a man writing by lamplight. The first entry was dated 3 March 1926.

Arrived Strandarklettur. Tower construction 80% complete. Danish engineers still on site. Sea moderate. Installed mercury bath — 90.4 kg by scale. Lens assembly to follow.

Ævar read the logbook in a single sitting, seven hours, cross-legged on the floor of the second landing with his back against the corroded panel. The keeper's name was Björn Halldórsson. He was methodical. He recorded wind speed, barometric pressure, visibility, sea state, and lamp hours with a consistency that Ævar recognized as either professionalism or compulsion. By April 1926, Björn was also recording the colour of the light.

14/04/26. Lamp nominal. Light: white. Clean.

09/07/26. Lamp nominal. Light: white. Clean.

22/10/26. Lamp nominal. Light: faint amber tint? Examined envelope — minor deposit, smaller than a fingernail. Will monitor.

Ævar held the calibration card against the page, uselessly, and then put it back in his breast pocket.

By December 1926, Björn's entries had changed. Not in content — he still recorded his numbers faithfully — but in what surrounded them. Between the data, in the margins, in the spaces where a disciplined man would have left silence, Björn had begun to write about the pressure.

03/12/26. Lamp off for cleaning, 14 minutes. Chest tightness began at minute 2. Peripheral vision reduced. Resumed lamp. Symptoms resolved within 30 seconds.

07/12/26. Contactor fault, lamp off 9 minutes. Pressure in sternum, distinct. As if being leaned against. Difficult to explain. Sea was dead calm throughout.

10/12/26. Examined envelope. Bloom now 60% coverage. CT approx 2,400K by estimation. Colour matches — and here Björn had paused, the nib of his pen leaving a small pool of ink before continuing — colour matches the light from below the stack at night when sea is still.

Ævar read that entry three times. Then he went to the lantern room window and looked down.

The sea was logn. The basalt stack dropped vertically into water so clear and dark that it seemed to have no surface, only depth. He could see nothing below. But Björn had seen something. Björn, ninety-nine years before, standing at this same window, looking down at the same water, had seen an amber light rising from the Grímsey Graben four hundred meters beneath him, and had written it down in a logbook he then sealed behind a wall.

The last entry was December 11, 1927. The handwriting was unchanged — steady, careful, Danish-inflected. Björn recorded the wind (NW, 15 knots), the pressure (1,003 millibars), the visibility (2 nautical miles, snow), and the lamp hours (11,206 total). Then he wrote:

The new filament

The sentence stopped. Not trailed off — stopped, as if the hand holding the pen had been lifted cleanly away. The remaining pages were blank. Ævar counted them. Forty-seven.

* * *

November twenty-third. The bloom covered the envelope entirely now, and the light it produced was no longer white by any standard Ævar had been trained to recognise. He held the card up. Square three. 2,400 Kelvin. The whole beam. The Fresnel lens still turned on its mercury bath, still swept its rotation, but the light it cast across the sea was the colour of old brass, of a healing bruise, of the specific amber that neonatal nurses call bilirubin light, though it was not that either. It was the colour of a tungsten filament burning through its own housing.

He had called Dalvík four times. The low-pressure system had not passed north. It had stalled and deepened. Force ten from the northwest, sustained. No boats. No helicopter. The earliest possible resupply was the twenty-eighth. Five days. He had the spare bulb, but changing the bulb required shutting down the light for a minimum of twenty minutes — twelve minutes for the mercury bath to stabilize, four minutes to remove the old bulb from the lamp changer, two minutes to seat the new one, two minutes for the filament to reach operating temperature. Twenty minutes of darkness.

He had felt what seven minutes of darkness did.

On the twenty-fourth he stopped sleeping. Not by choice. He lay in the keeper's bunk on the third landing and listened to the sound the tower made at night — a low, pervasive tone that was not quite sound, that sat below hearing in the place where the body interprets vibration as mood. He had read about this. Vic Tandy, 1998, Coventry University. A researcher who discovered that a standing wave at approximately 18.98 hertz — just below the threshold of human hearing — could cause chest pressure, peripheral vision disturbance, and a sense of presence. The source in Tandy's case was a defective extractor fan. The source here was four hundred meters of water and whatever lay beneath it, pulsing its slow geology against the base of the stack, sending its frequency up through twenty-three meters of cast iron and into the bones of Ævar's sternum.

He lay in the bunk and felt it lean against him. Not a creature. He would not call it a creature. A process. A thermal gradient in the Grímsey Graben, where the Tjörnes Fracture Zone pulled the earth apart in slow motion and high-temperature hydrothermal vents heated the sediment and the anhydrite deposits that sealed the system sat at some critical threshold betweenite and something else. He had looked this up before taking the posting — not because he cared about geology, but because counting the facts about a place was easier than feeling the reasons he had come there. The Graben was a pull-apart basin. The anhydrite was calcium sulphate. It was stable below 250 degrees Celsius. Above that temperature it dissolved, and the calcium and sulphate ions entered the water column, and whatever equilibrium the basin maintained would shift.

The light held it below 250. He did not know how. He did not need to know how. He was a nurse, not a geologist. He knew that the overhead warmer in an isolette held a premature infant's core temperature at 36.5 degrees, and he did not need to understand thermodynamics to know that when the warmer failed, the infant cooled, and when the infant cooled, the infant died. The mechanism was not the point. The threshold was the point. The threshold was always the point.

The light was amber and the thing below was amber and the threshold was dissolving at the same rate as the tungsten on the quartz.

* * *

On the twenty-fifth he began to write a letter to Líf. This was not unusual. He had written her eleven letters since her death, all unsent, all stored in the same envelope in his duffel bag. He wrote them in the same cloth-bound notebook he used for the light log, on pages he tore out afterward, because the log was professional and the letters were not.

Líf.

The light here is 2,400 Kelvin. You would hate it. You always said amber light made you look jaundiced, and I always said that was because you were comparing yourself to the wrong reference standard, and you always said the right reference standard for how a person looks is how the person they love sees them, and I never had an answer for that because you were right and I am a man who calibrates skin colour against a card.

The bulb is dying. I have a spare. I'm afraid to change it.

I'm afraid of twenty minutes.

He stopped writing. He held the pen above the page for eleven seconds — he counted — and then set it down. He looked at his hands. They were the same hands. Short nails, two millimetres. Broad palms. The extensor tendons stood out when he spread his fingers, and between the second and third knuckle of his left hand there was a small scar from an isolette latch that had caught him in his third year at Landspítali. He had placed the IV anyway, one-handed, while a colleague applied pressure to the cut with a gauze pad. The child's name was Þórdís. She had weighed 740 grams. She was nineteen now, presumably. He did not keep track.

His hands could do this. Twenty minutes. Twelve for the mercury, four for the extraction, two for the seating, two for warmup. He had done more precise work in worse conditions. He had intubated a 600-gram infant in an ambulance on the Keflavík highway during a snowstorm while the vehicle braked and the monitor screamed its seven-second alarm and his hands did not shake. His hands did not shake.

He went to the equipment locker and held the spare bulb. He turned it in his fingers. He checked the filament through the clear quartz — a tight coil, clean, unblackened. He checked the base pins. He checked the gas fill markings. Then he checked the date stamp again. 2019. Seven years old. Within tolerance but not new. Not new.

He put the bulb back and climbed the seventy-six steps to the lantern room and sat on the floor beside the mercury bath and listened to the lens turn above him, the brass gears meshing in their slow rotation, the light sweeping its amber arm across a sea that had been dead calm for three days.

* * *

November twenty-seventh. The filament was visibly deformed. He could see it through the bloom — a slight bow in the lower coil where the tungsten had thinned, the wire losing mass to the envelope grain by grain, atom by atom, the same process that killed every incandescent light source ever made: evaporation, deposition, thinning, failure. The halogen cycle was supposed to prevent this. The iodine or bromine gas inside the envelope was supposed to combine with the deposited tungsten and carry it back to the filament, a closed loop, a self-healing system. But the gas couldn't keep up. The filament was losing faster than the gas could return, and the bloom was the evidence, and the evidence was the colour of everything Ævar could no longer bear to look at.

The wind dropped at 14:00. By 15:00 the sea was logn. By 16:00 — and there was no 16:00, not really, not at this latitude in late November; there was only a thickening of the dark from charcoal to ink — by sixteen hundred, Ævar was standing in the lantern room with his forehead against the cold glass, looking down.

He saw it.

Not as Björn had described it — not a light, exactly. A luminance. A warmth in the colour spectrum that had no business being four hundred meters deep in the Arctic Ocean in November. It was diffuse, wide, sourceless, as if the sediment itself were running a low fever. Amber. He held the calibration card against the window. The glow matched the bloom. Square three. 2,400 Kelvin. The same colour above him and below him, as if the bulb and the deep were two ends of the same dying filament.

He pressed his hand flat against the glass. He could feel his radial pulse in his fingertips — seventy-one beats per minute, interval point eight four seconds. The pulse was steady. His hand was steady. Below him the amber glow pulsed at a rate he could not count because it was not a rate, it was a pressure, a leaning, the infrasound pushing through the glass and the iron and the basalt and the water and arriving in his sternum like a hand laid flat.

He pulled back from the window. He checked the bulb. The filament's bow had deepened. He estimated — and he was good at estimation, fourteen years of judging an infant's respiratory effort by the depth of subcostal retractions had made him good — that the filament would hold for another thirty to forty hours. Maybe less if the ambient temperature in the lantern room dropped further. The forecast called for minus twelve by the twenty-ninth.

He went down the seventy-six steps. At the fourth landing he stopped and looked out the east window. The sea was black and flat and the amber glow was not visible from this angle, and he stood there for two hundred and twelve seconds — he counted — until he was certain it had not followed him down.

* * *

On the twenty-eighth the supply boat did not come. Force eight, northwest. The radio operator in Dalvík said possibly the thirtieth. Possibly.

Ævar sat in the keeper's quarters on the third landing and read Björn's logbook for the fourth time. He had memorized passages. He recited them under his breath while he ate rice — two hundred and seven grains — and drank coffee from a tin cup that held exactly two hundred and forty millilitres.

Björn had been a fisherman before he was a keeper. He had come to Strandarklettur because his wife Vigdís had died of tuberculosis in 1925 and he could not stay in their house in Akureyri. He had written this in a single sentence on the first page of the logbook, without elaboration, in the manner of a man who understood that some facts require no context. Vigdís died 14 March 1925. I came here. Ævar touched the ink of that sentence with his fingertip. It was smooth and cool and ninety-nine years old, which was almost a hundred but not quite, and that gap — that single missing year — felt like the eight seconds between Líf's car entering the intersection and the signal changing, like the forty-two seconds between the alarm and his arrival at the bedside, like all the distances in his life that were almost enough but never were.

He turned to the last entry. The new filament — and then nothing. Forty-seven blank pages.

He imagined Björn's hand lifting from the page. He imagined the pen, a dip pen, the nib wet with ink, rising from the paper into the air of the lantern room. He imagined the sound the tower must have made that night — December 11, 1927, the eleventh of the twelfth — when whatever Björn was about to describe happened. Or stopped happening.

The new filament. Björn had been changing the bulb. He had received a replacement — there must have been a supply delivery on or before the eleventh — and he had shut down the light and begun the process, and in the twenty minutes of darkness something had shifted, and Björn had restarted the new bulb and sat down to record the event and written three words and then

Ævar closed the logbook. He pressed his palm flat against its cover and felt his radial pulse and counted. Seventy-three. Seventy-one. Seventy-four. Seventy-two.

He climbed the steps to the lantern room. Seventy-six. The bloom covered the envelope completely now. The filament glowed through it like a vein seen through jaundiced skin. He held up the calibration card and the card itself was amber in the amber light and the squares were impossible to differentiate and he realized that he had lost his reference standard.

* * *

November twenty-ninth. Minus fourteen. The filament had developed a second bow, higher on the coil, and between the two deformed sections the wire was visibly thinner than the leads. He estimated twelve hours. Perhaps eight.

He brought the spare bulb to the lantern room and set it on the floor beside the mercury bath. He laid out his tools: insulated gloves, socket wrench, alignment pins, a length of lint-free cloth. He arranged them in the order he would need them. He rehearsed the sequence. Contactor off. Wait for filament to cool — ninety seconds. Release the lamp changer clamp — two turns counterclockwise. Extract the bulb — pull straight down, do not twist. Seat the new bulb — align the pins, push until the base clicks. Tighten the clamp — two turns clockwise. Contactor on. Twenty minutes total. He could do it in less. He could do it in eighteen if his hands were fast. Seventeen if the mercury bath settled quickly.

He sat beside his tools and looked at the spare bulb. Through the clear quartz he could see the filament, a perfect coil, unbowed, unblackened, full of all the light it had not yet given. He thought of the isolettes at Landspítali, the rows of them in the NICU, each one a small glass house containing a life that might or might not continue, each one warmed to 36.5 degrees by a mechanism no infant understood or needed to understand. He thought of his hands adjusting the warmers. He thought of the overhead lights, their colour temperatures, the calibration card he carried because it was the last artifact of a life in which precision had meant something, in which the difference between 3,200 and 2,400 Kelvin was the difference between seeing clearly and seeing through amber, between reading a child's skin and misreading it, between catching the signs and missing them by eight seconds.

At 22:00 the filament in the dying bulb shifted. He saw it from across the lantern room — a visible sag, the lower coil dropping a millimetre, maybe two. The light dimmed. The bloom deepened. He held up the calibration card and the light was below square three now, below 2,400, somewhere in the range where tungsten stopped being a lamp and started being a memory of a lamp.

He felt the pressure begin. Not in his chest this time. In his wrists. In the tendons of his hands. As if something were pressing on the bones from outside, testing the architecture of the one instrument he had left.

He picked up the insulated gloves. He put them on. He flexed his fingers inside them — one, two, three, four, five, five, four, three, two, one. He counted the flex. He counted the seconds between each flex. Point six. Point six. Point seven. Point six.

He stood up and put his hand on the contactor.

* * *

He waited. He did not throw the switch. He stood with his hand on the contactor for four hundred and twelve seconds — six minutes and fifty-two seconds — while the filament sagged and the bloom deepened and the amber light turned the lantern room into a space he recognized from fourteen years of night shifts: the colour of a room where something is failing.

Below him the sea was logn. He could not see the amber glow from inside the lantern room because the light was still on, but he knew it was there. He could feel it in the frequency that came up through the iron and the basalt and the water, the nineteen hertz that Vic Tandy had identified as the resonant frequency of the human eyeball, the frequency at which the eye vibrated in its socket and produced peripheral distortions that the brain interpreted as presence. He was not haunted. He was resonating.

He thought of Björn's hand lifting from the page. He thought of the forty-seven blank pages. He thought: Björn changed the bulb. The new filament caught. The light came back. Björn sat down and wrote three words and then could not write any more, because what he had felt in those twenty minutes of darkness was not something that could be completed as a sentence. The sentence stopped because the experience stopped the capacity for sentences. And Björn had sealed the logbook behind the wall and lived — lived how long? The records would say. Ævar had not checked the records. He had not wanted to know how long a man could continue after an unfinishable sentence.

The filament sagged again. A sound came from it — a faint tick, metal cooling unevenly, a sound Ævar knew from the isolettes when the warmers cycled off. Tick. Tick. The intervals were irregular. Point three, point seven, point four, point nine. No rhythm. A dying filament did not keep time.

He threw the contactor.

* * *

The lantern room went dark. The Fresnel lens, still turning on its mercury bath, caught the aurora for one half-rotation and scattered green light across the walls, and then the mercury settled and the lens stopped and there was no light at all.

Ævar reached for the old bulb. His hands in the insulated gloves found the lamp changer clamp by touch — he had rehearsed this, he knew the distances, twelve centimetres from the contactor switch to the clamp, he had measured — and he turned it counterclockwise, two turns, and felt it release. He pulled the bulb straight down. It came free. It was hot through the gloves, hot enough that he could feel the bloom as a texture, the deposited tungsten roughening the quartz like scale on a kettle. He set it on the floor.

The pressure arrived.

It was immediate and total. Not a lean this time — a weight. As if the entire water column below the stack, four hundred meters of North Atlantic, had decided to express itself as a force applied directly to the centre of his chest. His breath shortened. His peripheral vision collapsed to a tunnel. He felt his pulse in his throat, in his temples, in the fingertips that were reaching for the spare bulb, and the pulse was fast now, ninety, ninety-five, one hundred, the intervals shrinking, point six, point five eight, point five five, and he was counting them because he could not stop counting them, because counting was the scar tissue over the wound of March 14th, 2019, when he had counted forty-two seconds and eight of them had been too many.

He seated the new bulb. Pins aligned. Push until the base clicks. The click came. He tightened the clamp — two turns clockwise. His hands did not shake. His hands never shook. They were the hands that had placed four thousand and eleven IVs, the hands that had intubated a 600-gram infant on the Keflavík highway, the hands that had held Líf's hand in the emergency department three floors below the NICU while the monitor beside her bed showed a rhythm he could read and she could not, and he had counted the intervals between the complexes and known, before the doctor spoke, that the intervals were widening, that the rhythm was failing, that the woman whose hand he held was going to a place where his counting could not follow.

He threw the contactor.

The cold filament received 240 volts. In a healthy bulb, the inrush current would heat the coil evenly, the resistance rising with temperature until the filament reached its operating point in one point eight seconds. But this was the spare bulb, the 2019 bulb, seven years in storage, and cold ignition was the most violent moment in a tungsten filament's life — the current at its highest, the resistance at its lowest, the thermal stress at its peak. The filament arced. A bright blue-white flash, 6,000 Kelvin, brighter than any light Ævar had seen since summer. The filament burned for a quarter-second at a colour temperature that matched the D65 square on his calibration card, the square he had used for fourteen years to check that the light on a child's skin was true.

Then the quartz envelope exploded.

It did not shatter. It fractured along the bloom line of the old deposits that had contaminated the lamp changer socket and transferred to the new bulb's base during installation — he had not cleaned the socket; he had been counting his pulse instead of cleaning the socket — and the fracture propagated from the base to the crown in a single crack that opened the envelope like a mouth. A shard of quartz, superheated, spinning, struck the inside of his right wrist at the point where the radial artery ran closest to the skin. The pain was white. He pulled his hand back and the shard stayed embedded and the blood came immediately, dark in the darkness, because the lantern room was dark again, completely dark, the filament dead, the spare dead, both bulbs gone.

Dark.

The pressure hit him like a wall. He staggered back against the lantern room glass. The nineteen hertz was not a frequency now, it was a physical thing, it was in the room with him, it was leaning against his sternum with the weight of four hundred meters of ocean, and his peripheral vision was gone entirely, and his breath was shallow, and his hands — his precise, professional, ruined hands — were hot with blood, the right wrist swelling around the embedded quartz, the tissues filling with fluid that pressed against the radial artery and obliterated the pulse he had been counting since the moment he understood that counting was the only thing between him and the sound Líf's monitor had made when the intervals stopped.

He could not feel his pulse.

He pressed his left thumb to his right wrist — the gesture so automatic, so deeply grooved by decades of clinical practice, that he did it without deciding — and felt nothing. Swelling. Heat. The wetness of blood. But no pulse. The artery was there, it was beating, it had to be beating, but the tissue pressure was too high and his thumb was too slick and for the first time in his life his hands could not find the rhythm.

He pressed harder. Nothing.

He moved his thumb. Proximal, distal, lateral, medial. The four quadrants of a radial pulse check, the first skill taught in nursing school, the skill you learn before you learn anything else because if you cannot find a pulse you cannot begin. He could not find it.

The pressure in his chest eased slightly, or he stopped noticing it, because the absence of his own pulse was louder than any infrasound. He was standing in a dark lantern room on a basalt sea stack two point one kilometres off the only inhabited island on the Arctic Circle, and both bulbs were dead, and the sea was logn, and below him something was changing.

He looked down through the lantern room glass.

The amber glow was rising. It was no longer a diffuse luminance at depth. It had a shape now — not a body, not a form, but a boundary, a thermal front, a zone of water that was warmer than the water around it, and the warmth was visible as colour, and the colour was amber, and it was wider than the tower's foundation. It was wider than the stack. It rose slowly, the way a thermocline shifts, the way warm water displaces cold when a system crosses its threshold and the anhydrite dissolves and the calcium and sulphate enter the column and the equilibrium that held for ninety-nine years lets go.

Ævar closed his eyes.

He could not count. For the first time since March 14th, 2019 — since alarm forty-two, since the forty-two seconds, since the eight-second gap between a living wife and a dead one — he could not count, because counting required a pulse and his pulse was buried under blood and swelling and a shard of quartz from a bulb he had failed to seat in a socket he had failed to clean in a tower he had come to because he thought being alone would be the same as being forgiven.

The calibration card was in his breast pocket. He felt it there against his chest, a small rectangle of coated paper with its squares of colour from 2,000 to 6,500 Kelvin, the spectrum from deep red to cool blue, the range within which a human life could be accurately read. His right hand was useless. His left hand moved to the pocket — muscle memory, the body's insistence on procedure even when procedure has no object — and his fingers found the card's edge and drew it out and held it.

He opened his eyes. The amber glow was closer. He could see it through the floor grate now, through the seventy-six steps' worth of cast iron, a light coming up through the structure of the tower itself, as if the basalt and the iron and the mercury were all conducting it, passing it upward, grain by grain, atom by atom, the way the tungsten had moved from the filament to the envelope, a slow deposition, a transfer of energy from one state to another.

His fingers opened. The card slipped from between them — not dropped, released, the way you release a breath you've been holding too long, the way you release a pulse you've been counting because counting was the only alternative to grief and grief was the only alternative to silence and silence was what Björn had found on December 11, 1927, the silence of a sentence that cannot be finished because what comes after the last word is not a word.

The card fell. It caught the amber light as it passed through the floor grate, a small white rectangle turning gold, turning the colour of everything Ævar had measured and everything he had missed, and it dropped through the iron lattice toward the base of the tower, toward the sea, toward something that was, for the first time in a very long time, moving.

He stood in the dark with his hands at his sides. The right one bled. The left one was empty. Below him the logn broke, and the water, which had been so still, began to breathe.
