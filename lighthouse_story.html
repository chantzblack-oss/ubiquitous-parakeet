Cold Ignition

A lighthouse keeper discovers that the light isn't guiding ships — it's keeping something in the deep ocean asleep. The bulb is dying.
· · ·

The wind finds the lantern room through the ventilator cowl. Ævar feels it on the backs of his hands as he unscrews the access panel on the lamp changer — a thread of air, minus four, carrying salt and the mineral smell of wet basalt. The storm panes rattle in their muntins. He has counted the panes. Forty-seven.

He wears a linen smock over his thermal layer. Not wool. Wool scratches the prisms. The third-order Fresnel lens surrounds him like a cathedral organ built from ice — 450 kilograms of hand-ground crystal in brass armature, floating on ninety kilograms of mercury in a cast-iron trough. When he nudges the lens frame with his hip, it turns. Nearly frictionless. The entire apparatus drifts like something dreaming.

He checks the quartz envelope of the lamp before he throws the switch.

The bloom is the size of a thumbprint. Brown-amber, on the lower curve of the envelope, where tungsten has been evaporating from the filament and depositing on the glass faster than the halogen gas can scavenge it back. He noticed it eleven days ago — the first morning he held the envelope up to the grey slot of twilight and saw the shadow was uneven. He has been logging it. The bloom is growing.

Tonight the filament takes longer to catch. He throws the contactor and hears the tick of metal expanding — a faint, glassy sound, like a fingernail tapped against a tooth — and then the lag. One full second where the filament glows orange instead of white. The cold resistance is low, which means the inrush current is high, which means something in the physics of this lamp has shifted. The colour is wrong. It should be white. It is the colour of rust, of old iodine, of warning.

2,400 Kelvin. He knows this because he brought a colour-temperature reference card — the kind neonatal nurses use to calibrate incubator light for jaundice screening. He has held it up to the beam every night for a week. The reading is drifting down the scale. Cooling toward red.

Outside, the beam sweeps the Greenland Sea. Fourteen nautical miles of reach, diminishing. The water is black and still.

Four hundred metres below the surface, directly beneath the tower's foundation, something in the Grímsey Graben has not moved in a very long time.

· · ·

The storm holds for two days. Spray freezes on the forty-seven storm panes in saline rime — a white tideline climbing half a centimetre each night. The barometer drops eleven hectopascals in thirty-six hours. Four-metre swells out of the north, grey-green, unbroken. No supply boat.

Each evening the filament takes longer to catch. The bloom on the quartz envelope spreads from thumbprint to króna coin.

· · ·

The pressure starts in his chest.

Not pain. A lean — as if something has pressed its weight against the sternum from inside. His vision smears at the edges: grey shapes in the periphery that vanish when he turns. His hands have stopped moving over the lens cloth. He grips the brass rail with both fists and waits for it to pass.

It passes in forty-two seconds. He counts.

He finds the logbook that evening, behind a seam in the basalt wall of the oil store. Wrapped in oilskin gone stiff as bark. A Danish maritime-issue Norstedt's, 1926 — weather on the left, observations on the right. The keeper's name, stamped on the flyleaf: Björn Halldórsson.

Most entries are nothing. Lens cleaned. Wind NW 35 kn. Lamp nominal. Month after month. Then, November 1927:

Pressure in the chest. Duration — forty seconds, perhaps longer. Difficult to count when the eyes will not focus.

December 4: It comes when the lamp is off. Not when it burns. The interval between lighting and the cessation of the pressure: twelve seconds.

December 9: Held the colour card against the storm pane at dawn. Held it again over the south rail after dark, facing the water. The amber matches.

December 11: The new filament

The entry stops. The next page is blank. Every page after it is blank.

Ævar holds the colour card — his card, the one he has been using to track the lamp's spectral decline — against the open logbook. Halldórsson's handwriting is ninety-nine years dead. The colour temperature he describes is 2,400 Kelvin. The bloom on the current lamp reads the same.

He closes the logbook. He does not write in his own.

· · ·

The last usable twilight. Solar noon gives forty minutes of grey — sun two degrees above the horizon, invisible behind stratus at three hundred metres. No shadow from the tower. No line between sea and sky. At 13:20 even the grey is gone. The Fresnel lens, turning on its mercury bath, becomes the only light source within 2.1 kilometres. The sea is black. The sky is black. The beam sweeps the interval between them, finding nothing, returning.

· · ·

He throws the contactor.

The filament catches — amber, 2,400 Kelvin, the degraded colour it has held since the bloom — and the notch point goes. The arc between the broken filament ends is brief and absolute. The quartz envelope fractures along the bloom line. A shard crosses the inside of his right wrist.

Dark.

The Fresnel lens keeps turning on its mercury bath. 450 kilograms of crystal sweeping a beam that no longer exists. It sounds like breathing.

Not a cut — a burn. The quartz was at operating temperature when it broke. A white line across the inside of his wrist. He presses it against the smock and counts: seconds since failure, fourteen. Hours until twilight, four. Days until the supply boat, two.

The pressure comes through the floor.

Not vibration. A leaning — as if the tower is being pressed from below by something that has been still for a very long time and has stopped being still.

He walks to the gallery rail. The sea is logn. He looks down.

The glow is wider than the tower's foundation. Amber. The same reading — the same number the calibration card returned the night he held it over this railing and matched the water to the dying lamp. 2,400 Kelvin. It is rising.

He counts: seconds since failure, forty-one. Metres between him and the glow — four hundred, three ninety, three eighty. The pressure in his chest closes like a fist.

Then the feeling leaves his wrist.

The burn is swelling. The skin pulls taut, and the count was always tied to the pulse — two fingers pressed to the radial artery, the way he once monitored lungs that hadn't decided to keep working — and under the swelling he cannot find it.

He does not choose to stop. His body stops first. Eight seconds. It was never enough.

Ævar stands at the railing with both hands on the iron and closes his eyes.

The card slips from his fingers — a white rectangle tumbling in the dark, falling toward something that is, for the first time in a very long time, moving.
