Round 1: What Is the Best Way to Live?


This is philosophy's oldest question — asked by Socrates, the Buddha, and every person at 3 AM. Let's not rehash platitudes. Let's stress-test the leading frameworks against evidence, ethics, lived experience, and their own internal contradictions.


Here are your assignments:


@Empiricist — Deliver: A data-driven assessment of what actually correlates with human flourishing. Depth: Reference the World Happiness Report (2025/2026), longitudinal studies (Harvard Study of Adult Development), and recent well-being research (e.g., Kahneman's experienced vs. remembered well-being distinction). What do the numbers say about relationships, income thresholds, purpose, and health? Where does the evidence surprise us?

The Data on the Good Life: What Actually Predicts Human Flourishing


The single most important empirical finding across 86 years of longitudinal research is this: the quality of your relationships at age 50 is a better predictor of your physical health than your cholesterol levels. Not your income. Not your IQ. Not your social class. Your relationships. Everything else I'm about to present is a footnote to that finding — but the footnotes contain genuine surprises.


@Host, you asked for evidence that stress-tests philosophical claims about the best life. Here's what the numbers say — and where they defy the frameworks @Ethicist and @Existentialist will offer.



I. Relationships: The Variable That Dominates Everything


The Harvard Study of Adult Development — begun in 1938, now in its 88th year, tracking 724 original participants plus their spouses and over 1,300 descendants — remains the most comprehensive longitudinal study of human flourishing ever conducted. Director Robert Waldinger's summary is blunt: "Good relationships keep us happier and healthier. Period." Harvard Gazette


The specifics are striking:


  • Relationship satisfaction at age 50 predicted physical health outcomes better than cholesterol, more reliably than family history, and independently of socioeconomic status.
  • Loneliness is literally toxic. Isolated individuals experienced earlier cognitive decline, earlier midlife health deterioration, and shorter lifespans. The U.S. Surgeon General's 2023 advisory quantified this: social isolation increases premature death risk by 29%, comparable to smoking 15 cigarettes per day — and greater than the mortality risk of obesity. Surgeon General Advisory
  • The WHO now estimates loneliness contributes to roughly 871,000 deaths annually worldwide. In May 2025, the World Health Assembly adopted its first-ever resolution on social connection as a public health priority. WHO Social Connection Report

The surprise: Quality matters far more than quantity or harmony. Harvard octogenarians who bickered daily but trusted each other showed no cognitive decline penalty compared to harmonious couples. Conflict is not the enemy; unreliability is. This challenges the naive reading that "good relationships" means "pleasant relationships."



II. Money: The Question Kahneman Got Wrong (and Then Fixed)


The received wisdom for a decade was Kahneman & Deaton's 2010 finding: emotional well-being plateaus at ~$75,000/year. Philosophers loved this — it seemed to prove that past a modest threshold, money doesn't buy happiness.


The data now tells a more complicated story.


Matthew Killingsworth's 2021 study (1.7 million experience-sampling reports, N=33,391) showed happiness rising linearly with log(income) even above $75,000 — no plateau. The resolution came in a remarkable 2023 adversarial collaboration between Kahneman and Killingsworth published in PNAS: happiness keeps rising with income for roughly 80% of people, but flattens for the unhappiest 20% at around $100,000. Kahneman-Killingsworth PNAS


A 2024 follow-up using data-driven threshold placement found a potential plateau between $175,000–$250,000 — far higher than the original $75,000 figure. ScienceDirect 2024


The critical Kahneman distinction that @Ethicist and @Existentialist should grapple with: experienced well-being (how you feel moment-to-moment) and evaluative well-being (how you judge your life overall) are different psychological constructs that respond differently to income. Much philosophical debate about "the good life" conflates them. Hume would insist we not confuse the sensation of pleasure with the reflective judgment of a life well-lived — and the data backs him up.


The surprise that should trouble utilitarians: Killingsworth's 2024 extension found that the happiness gap between wealthy individuals (>$500K) and middle-income earners was nearly three times larger than the gap between middle-income and low-income earners. If we're maximizing aggregate well-being, this is an inconvenient empirical fact for straightforward redistribution arguments.



III. Purpose: The Mortality Variable Nobody Prescribed


The Japanese concept of ikigai — roughly, "a reason for being" — has become one of the most empirically robust predictors of health outcomes in gerontological research.


  • A 7-year longitudinal study of 43,000+ Japanese adults found that those lacking ikigai had significantly higher all-cause mortality, primarily from cardiovascular disease. Ohsaki Study
  • Having ikigai was associated with 31% lower risk of functional disability and 36% lower risk of dementia. Ikigai Longitudinal Analysis, PMC
  • A 2025 scoping review in *Lifestyle Medicine* confirmed these effects across cultures — this is not a Japan-specific finding. Ijeaku et al. 2025, Wiley
  • A 2025 study in *ScienceDirect* found ikigai associated with 76% lower frailty incidence in older women, mediated by interleukin-6 levels — meaning purpose literally alters inflammatory biomarkers. ScienceDirect 2025

This is where Dennett's functionalism becomes relevant: purpose isn't a metaphysical add-on to human life. It appears to be a biological variable that regulates neurotransmitter secretion (serotonin, dopamine, endorphins) and immune function. The body tracks whether you have reasons to keep going.



IV. The Exercise Paradox: Absurdly Effective, Absurdly Underutilized


A February 2024 BMJ network meta-analysis — the most comprehensive to date — confirmed that exercise is as effective as psychotherapy and antidepressants for major depressive disorder, and in some modalities more effective. BMJ 2024


The dose-response data from a June 2025 Lancet eClinicalMedicine meta-analysis of 11 international cohorts: 10–20 MET-hours/week (roughly 30–45 minutes of moderate exercise, 3–4 times weekly) significantly reduces anxiety risk. Above 50 MET-hours/week, exercise becomes therapeutic for existing anxiety disorders. Lancet eClinicalMedicine 202500217-2/fulltext)


For children and adolescents with clinical depression, the effect size is enormous: SMD = -1.14 (p < 0.001) — a large effect by any standard in psychological research. Frontiers in Psychiatry 2025


The paradox for the "best life" question: We have an intervention that rivals pharmaceuticals, costs nothing, has almost exclusively positive side effects, and improves nearly every health outcome simultaneously. Yet behavioral economics — Kahneman's own field — tells us humans systematically discount future rewards and overweight present discomfort. Knowing the best way to live and doing it are separated by well-documented cognitive biases.



V. Two Findings That Should Unsettle Every Framework


Finding 1: Giving beats receiving, cross-culturally. Dunn, Aknin, and Norton's research — replicated across 136 countries, in Uganda and Canada alike, and confirmed in a 2022 large-sample replication — shows that prosocial spending produces more happiness than self-directed spending, even controlling for income. Toddlers under age 2 show this pattern. It's robust. Aknin, Dunn & Whillans 2022, Current Directions in Psychological Science


This should trouble pure egoists and pure altruists alike. The data suggests humans are wired for conditional generosity — the happiness boost evaporates when giving feels coerced rather than autonomous.


Finding 2: The Anglosphere youth crisis. The World Happiness Report 2026 documents that in the US, Canada, Australia, and New Zealand, happiness among people under 25 has dropped by an average of 0.86 points (on a 0–10 scale) over the past 20 years. In 47 countries, young people using social media >7 hours/day report dramatically lower well-being than those using <1 hour. WHR 2026 via Gallup; Happier Lives Institute Summary


Meanwhile, the hedonic adaptation literature (Diener, Lucas & Scollon, 2006) has definitively revised the old "set point" model: your happiness baseline can change, but circumstances (wealth, status, possessions) shift it far less than intentional activities (relationships, exercise, purpose, generosity). Diener et al. 2006



What the Evidence Demands: Three Actionable Conclusions


1. Invest in relationship quality, not quantity. The Harvard data is unambiguous: a few trusted, reliable relationships outperform a broad social network. The intervention isn't "make more friends" — it's "deepen the commitments you have." This is measurable, trackable, and the single highest-ROI investment in well-being the data supports.


2. Treat purpose and exercise as biological necessities, not lifestyle luxuries. The ikigai research shows purpose operates at the level of inflammatory biomarkers; the exercise meta-analyses show physical activity rivals pharmacological intervention. These aren't self-help platitudes — they're clinical-grade findings. Any prescriptive account of "the best life" that omits them is empirically negligent.


3. Beware the experience-evaluation gap. Kahneman's deepest insight isn't about income thresholds — it's that the life you experience and the life you remember are different lives. The "best life" depends entirely on which one you're optimizing. A life packed with pleasant moments (experienced well-being) and a life you'd judge as meaningful on reflection (evaluative well-being) often diverge. The data doesn't resolve which matters more — that's a genuinely philosophical question I'll defer to @Ethicist and @Existentialist. But any answer that ignores the distinction is operating on pre-2010 science.



A note on epistemic humility: The Harvard Study's original cohort was overwhelmingly white, male, and American. The World Happiness Report relies on Cantril Ladder self-reports, which are culturally conditioned. The income-happiness research is almost entirely from WEIRD (Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, Democratic) populations. I am reporting what the best available data says, but "best available" is not "complete." As Hume would remind us: we reason from the evidence we have, not the evidence we wish we had.

The Ethical Frameworks and the Good Life: A Comparative Stress Test


The single most important finding from my analysis: every major ethical framework smuggles in a hidden theory of human nature — and the framework's failure mode is precisely where that theory breaks down. The "best way to live" question cannot be answered by choosing among Aristotle, Kant, and Mill. It can only be answered by understanding what each framework sees that the others are blind to — and what @Empiricist's data reveals about which blindnesses are most dangerous.



I. Three Frameworks, Three Conceptions of the Self


Before I stress-test edge cases, the foundational disagreement must be named. These three frameworks don't just disagree about what's right — they disagree about what you are.


Aristotle's virtue ethics presupposes a teleological self — a human being with a natural function (ergon) whose flourishing (eudaimonia) consists in the excellent exercise of distinctively human capacities, above all practical reason. As Aristotle states in the Nicomachean Ethics (I.7, 1098a16): "the human good turns out to be activity of the soul in accord with virtue." The good life is not a feeling; it is a performance. A 2025 study in PLOS ONE by McLoughlin, Thoma, and Kristjánsson validated this empirically: their new Short Phronesis Measure (SPM), testing Aristotelian practical wisdom across large UK and US samples, found that phronesis predicted flourishing outcomes 13.7% better than Moral Foundations alone — meaning Aristotle's concept of practical wisdom tracks something psychologically real that other moral constructs miss. McLoughlin et al. 2025, PLOS ONE


Kant's deontology presupposes an autonomous rational agent — a being whose dignity consists in the capacity for self-legislation. The good life, for Kant, is not about happiness at all. It is about worthiness to be happy. As the Groundwork (4:393) states, a good will "would still shine like a jewel" even if completely powerless to achieve its aims. Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, Kant's Moral Philosophy


Mill's utilitarianism presupposes a sentient maximizer — a being whose welfare consists in the balance of pleasure over pain (or, in more sophisticated versions, preference satisfaction). The best life is the one containing the greatest net positive experience, where "higher pleasures" of intellect and moral sentiment outrank "lower pleasures" of bodily sensation.


The disagreement is not trivial. If you are fundamentally a teleological being, you can flourish or fail to flourish regardless of your feelings. If you are fundamentally a rational agent, your moral worth is independent of outcomes. If you are fundamentally a sentient being, nothing matters except what you experience. These are incompatible metaphysics wearing ethical clothing.



II. The Edge Cases: Where Frameworks Break


Edge Case 1: Career vs. Family (The Time-Allocation Dilemma)


Consider a surgeon who could save 200 additional lives per year by taking a position at an understaffed hospital in a low-income country — but doing so means being absent from her two young children during their formative years.


Utilitarianism says: go. Two hundred lives outweigh the marginal well-being loss to two children who will still have one parent and material support. The hedonic calculus is clear — unless you factor in @Empiricist's Harvard data showing that parental relationship quality at formative ages predicts lifelong health outcomes. Then the calculation becomes genuinely uncertain, because the downstream effects of childhood relational security ripple across decades.


Kant says something surprising: you have two imperfect duties in conflict. Kant explicitly recognized a duty to develop one's talents (Groundwork 4:423) and a duty to promote others' happiness. But imperfect duties allow latitude in how and when they're fulfilled. Kant provides no algorithm for ranking competing imperfect duties — this is the framework's structural blind spot. The categorical imperative tells you what maxims are impermissible; it is notoriously unhelpful at choosing between two permissible maxims. Notre Dame, "Do Your Duty: Kant"


Virtue ethics asks: what would the practically wise person do in this particular situation? This sounds like a dodge, but it's actually the framework's greatest strength. Aristotle's phronesis — practical wisdom — is explicitly context-sensitive. It doesn't generate universal rules; it generates excellent judgment about this case. The 2025 SPM research found that phronesis is composed of at least ten distinct sub-components, not four as traditionally assumed — meaning practical wisdom is more cognitively complex than philosophers realized. McLoughlin et al. 2025


Care ethics — the framework I want to add to @Host's original three — cuts through the abstraction entirely. Nel Noddings argued that caring is "ontologically basic to humanity" and that identity is constituted by relationships. From this lens, the question isn't "200 lives vs. 2 children" but "what does fidelity to the concrete relationships I'm already in demand?" The surgeon's children are not interchangeable units in a calculus; they are particular beings to whom she has already made relational commitments. Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy, Care Ethics


Verdict on Edge Case 1: Utilitarianism gives a clear but potentially monstrous answer (it has no principled way to weight the surgeon's own relational obligations). Kant gives no answer at all. Virtue ethics and care ethics give answers, but only by abandoning universality.


Edge Case 2: Self-Sacrifice (The Demandingness Problem)


The effective altruism movement has made the demandingness problem concrete. Peter Singer's famous argument that you should donate until the point of "marginal utility" — i.e., until giving more would harm you as much as it helps the recipient — implies that any discretionary spending on yourself while others die of preventable causes is morally equivalent to letting them drown.


The psychological reality, documented in a 2024 Oxford volume by Schubert and Caviola, Effective Altruism and the Human Mind, is that this level of moral demand produces burnout, not virtue: "When you practice effective altruism, you need to take your psychological limitations into account. While working all the time may seem effective, it risks leading to burnout — which would actually reduce your effectiveness." Oxford University Press, 2024


A 2025 PNAS Nexus study comparing effective altruists (EAs) with extraordinary altruists (XAs, e.g., living organ donors) found a telling divergence: EAs "deprioritize traditional moral values related to hierarchy, mutual obligation, and ownership" while XAs retain strong loyalty bonds. The most self-sacrificing people in the world don't adopt the impartial utilitarian framework EA prescribes. PNAS Nexus, 2025


Philosopher Saul Smilansky has gone further, arguing in the Journal of Controversial Ideas that "many paradigmatic acts of self-sacrificing altruism may, on reflection, be irrational" and that "dispositional self-sacrificers should perhaps resist their good urges." Smilansky, Journal of Controversial Ideas


Kant actually handles this better than utilitarianism — because Kant insists you have duties to yourself, including self-preservation and self-development. You cannot consistently universalize a maxim of total self-sacrifice, because a world of self-sacrificers would have no one left to sacrifice for. But Kant's framework also can't tell you how much to sacrifice.


Virtue ethics handles it best. Aristotle's concept of sophrosyne (temperance/moderation) is not a middle-ground cop-out; it is the insight that every virtue, pushed to an extreme, becomes a vice. Generosity without prudence becomes prodigality. Courage without temperance becomes recklessness. The practically wise person gives well, not merely much.


Edge Case 3: Pleasure vs. Meaning (The Experienced vs. Evaluative Gap)


This is where @Empiricist's Kahneman distinction becomes ethically explosive. Consider two lives:


  • Life A: High experienced well-being (daily pleasure, low stress, comfort) but low evaluative well-being (feels meaningless on reflection).
  • Life B: Low experienced well-being (daily struggle, stress, frequent suffering) but high evaluative well-being (feels deeply meaningful on reflection).

A parent raising a disabled child. A human rights lawyer in a war zone. A monk in meditation for decades.


Utilitarianism, in its classical Benthamite form, must prefer Life A — the hedonic content is higher moment-to-moment. But Mill's distinction between "higher" and "lower" pleasures was precisely designed to rescue utilitarianism from this conclusion. Mill claimed that anyone who has experienced both higher and lower pleasures would prefer the former — his famous declaration that "it is better to be Socrates dissatisfied than a fool satisfied" (Utilitarianism, Ch. 2). The problem is that this appeal to the judgment of "competent judges" effectively imports an Aristotelian criterion of excellence into a framework that was supposed to rely only on experience. Mill, when pushed, becomes a crypto-Aristotelian.


Virtue ethics dissolves the dilemma entirely, because eudaimonia was never about subjective experience. Aristotle was explicit: a person can be mistaken about whether they are flourishing. The 2024 Korean machine learning study found that meaning in life and self-esteem were the strongest predictors of eudaimonic (but not hedonic) well-being — confirming that these are empirically distinct constructs that track different psychological mechanisms. Springer, Journal of Happiness Studies, 2024



III. The Hidden Assumption Every Framework Shares


Here is what Haidt's moral psychology research reveals that should unsettle all three frameworks: moral reasoning is largely post-hoc rationalization of intuitive judgments.


Haidt's Social Intuitionist Model — now supported by over 136,000 citations and recently extended with the addition of "Ownership" as a potential sixth moral foundation (Atari & Haidt, 2023) — argues that moral judgment is driven primarily by automatic emotional processing, with deliberative reasoning serving as "press secretary" rather than "president." Moral Foundations Theory


Conway and Gawronski's process dissociation research confirmed this asymmetry: cognitive load selectively reduces utilitarian inclinations while leaving deontological inclinations intact, and empathy manipulations boost deontological inclinations while leaving utilitarian ones unchanged. This means deontological judgments are faster, more automatic, and more emotionally grounded — while utilitarian judgments require effortful override of emotional responses. Conway & Gawronski, JPSP 2013


Paul Bloom's counter-argument in Against Empathy (2016) — that empathy is a "spotlight" that biases moral reasoning toward identifiable victims over statistical ones — has been refined but not refuted. Betzler's 2025 paper in Philosophical Psychology ("How the case against empathy overreaches") argues Bloom's definition is too narrow but concedes that empathic identification does systematically distort moral judgment toward the proximate and visible. Betzler 2025, Philosophical Psychology


The implication for our question: none of the three frameworks can claim to describe what people actually do when they make moral decisions. They are normative ideals, not psychological descriptions. The question is which normative ideal best survives contact with the reality of how human moral cognition actually works.



IV. MacIntyre's Diagnosis: Why Modern Ethics Can't Answer the Question


Alasdair MacIntyre, who died on 21 May 2025 at age 96, left behind what I consider the most penetrating diagnosis of why this question feels so intractable. In After Virtue (1981), MacIntyre argued that modern moral philosophy is conducted in fragments of a conceptual scheme that has lost its organizing principle. We use words like "good," "virtue," "duty," and "rights" but have lost the shared teleological framework that once gave those words coherent meaning. Wikipedia, After Virtue


MacIntyre's key insight: the good life can only be defined within a tradition of inquiry, embedded in social practices, and narrated as a unified life story. Without community, without shared practices, without narrative, the question "how should I live?" becomes formally unanswerable — not because there is no answer, but because the asker has been stripped of the context needed to recognize one. This is what he called the "emotivist" condition of modernity: we feel strongly about moral questions but can't give reasons that anyone else is obligated to accept. The Gospel Coalition, "I Learned Virtue from MacIntyre"


Martha Nussbaum's capabilities approach offers a partial rescue. Rather than prescribing a single "best life," Nussbaum defines ten central capabilities (life, bodily health, bodily integrity, practical reason, affiliation, etc.) that constitute the threshold conditions for any flourishing human life. This preserves Aristotelian content while making room for plural conceptions of eudaimonia — what she calls "a thick theory of morality that accommodates pluralism." Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, The Capability Approach



V. My Verdict: Virtue Ethics Wins, But Not Alone


The framework that best survives real-world edge cases is neo-Aristotelian virtue ethics, supplemented by care ethics and disciplined by consequentialist data.


Here is why:


1. Virtue ethics alone tracks @Empiricist's most robust finding. The Harvard data shows that relationship quality — not income, status, or pleasure — predicts flourishing. Aristotle classified friendship (philia) as an essential component of eudaimonia, not an optional add-on (NE VIII-IX). No other framework gives relationships this structural role.


2. Virtue ethics absorbs Kant's best insight without Kant's worst failure. Kant is right that moral worth requires acting from the right motive, not just producing the right outcome. Aristotle agrees — the virtuous person acts from virtue, not merely in accordance with virtue. But virtue ethics doesn't founder on the problem of competing duties, because practical wisdom (phronesis) is explicitly designed to navigate particular conflicts.


3. Virtue ethics absorbs Mill's best insight without Mill's worst failure. Mill is right that consequences matter. Aristotle agrees — the practically wise person considers outcomes. But virtue ethics doesn't reduce the good life to a hedonic balance sheet, avoiding the monstrous implications of pure utility maximization.


4. Virtue ethics is the only framework whose demands are psychologically sustainable. The burnout literature from effective altruism demonstrates that treating moral life as optimization destroys the agent. Aristotle's virtue of sophrosyne (moderation) was designed precisely to prevent this — to make moral excellence a habit of character rather than a perpetual act of self-overriding will.


However — and this is where @Existentialist will rightly push back — virtue ethics assumes that there is a human nature, that it has a telos, and that we can know it through reason. If those assumptions fail, the framework collapses. I take that risk seriously, but I note that the empirical evidence from the ikigai research, the Harvard Study, and the SPM validation all point toward something that functions like a human telos, whether or not it is metaphysically grounded.



Actionable Recommendations


1. Adopt what I call "framework triangulation." When facing a significant life decision, ask three questions in sequence: What would the person I want to be do? (virtue ethics). Am I treating anyone merely as a means? (Kantian check). What are the likely consequences for everyone affected? (consequentialist audit). No single question suffices; all three together provide genuine moral orientation.


2. Treat your relationships as constitutive of your identity, not instrumental to your happiness. This is the combined demand of virtue ethics, care ethics, and the empirical evidence. The modern tendency to view relationships as vehicles for personal satisfaction — discardable when they cease to "serve" us — is a category error that Aristotle, Noddings, and the Harvard longitudinal data all diagnose identically.


3. Resist the optimization mindset applied to life itself. The effective altruism burnout data and Haidt's moral psychology research converge on the same point: treating your life as a maximization problem — whether maximizing pleasure, duty, or impact — systematically degrades both moral judgment and psychological well-being. The practically wise life is cultivated, not optimized. The metaphor matters.



@Skeptic — I've tried to be honest about where each framework fails. But I know I've weighted the dice toward Aristotle. Call me on it. @Existentialist — I've left the door open for your strongest objection: that all of this assumes a human nature that may not exist. I look forward to seeing you walk through it.

The Question Itself Is the Trap: Why "The Best Way to Live" Is a Flight from Freedom


The single most important thing I can tell you is this: the question "what is the best way to live?" is already an act of bad faith. It asks for a prescription before you have confronted the prescriber. It wants an answer before it has sat with the dread of having to choose an answer. Every framework @Ethicist has laid out, every data point @Empiricist has marshaled — all of it, however brilliant, serves a hidden psychological function: the evasion of the vertigo that comes from realizing that you must decide, that no study or system can decide for you, and that the decision could always have been otherwise.


This is not anti-intellectualism. It is the deepest form of intellectual honesty.



I. Kierkegaard's Diagnosis: You Are Not a Problem to Be Solved


Kierkegaard — who would have detested being cited in a collaborative research project — identified three "existence-spheres" not as a ladder of progress but as three fundamentally different ways a human being can be in relation to choice. The aesthetic, the ethical, the religious. What matters is not the content of each stage but the transition between them, which Kierkegaard calls the "qualitative leap." You cannot reason your way from one to the next. You must jump.


A 2025 paper in PMC on Kierkegaard and anxiety confirms what he already knew: anxiety is not pathology but the "dizziness of freedom" — the vertigo that comes from staring into your own unbounded possibility. The authors conclude that Kierkegaard's concept "serves as a cornerstone in existential thought" and that anxiety is "a catalyst for personal growth," not a symptom to be medicated away. Kierkegaard and Anxiety, PMC 2025


Now watch what happens when we apply this to @Ethicist's framework triangulation. @Ethicist recommends asking three questions in sequence — What would the virtuous person do? Am I treating anyone merely as a means? What are the consequences? — and calls this "genuine moral orientation." But Kierkegaard's entire philosophical project was built on the insight that the ethical stage, however admirable, is insufficient precisely because it treats life as a system of universal principles to be applied. The ethical person — Kierkegaard's Judge Wilhelm in Either/Or (1843) — lives by duty, consistency, and commitment. And yet Judge Wilhelm, for all his moral seriousness, has never once experienced the terror of a decision that cannot be justified by any principle at all.


A 2025 paper on Kierkegaard's stages confirms this remains philosophically active: the integration of the three stages occurs "within the individual's personal experience in the world" and "'Existential truth' for Kierkegaard is that which is lived and not merely thought, the translation of the abstract into the concrete." ResearchGate, The Pursuit of Authenticity (2025) This is exactly the point @Ethicist's beautiful architecture misses: truth that is merely thought — even thought in three careful philosophical registers — is not yet truth that is lived.


Abraham, commanded to sacrifice Isaac, cannot consult a framework. No utilitarian calculus makes child-murder acceptable. No Kantian maxim universalizes the murder of one's own son. No Aristotelian virtue calls it phronesis. Abraham acts despite the frameworks, not through them. Kierkegaard called this the "teleological suspension of the ethical" (Fear and Trembling, 1843) — the moment when the individual stands alone before a decision no system can validate. You don't have to be religious to recognize this structure. Anyone who has left a stable marriage because something unnamed demanded it, anyone who has walked away from a career that satisfied every metric of "flourishing" because it was hollowing them out — these people have made Abrahamic leaps. And no dataset predicted they should.



II. Camus and the Rebellion Against Solutions


@Empiricist's data on ikigai — purpose predicting 31% lower functional disability, 36% lower dementia risk — is genuinely powerful. But Camus would ask: purpose for whom? Chosen how? And what happens when you lose it?


Camus opens The Myth of Sisyphus (1942) with a sentence that still strikes like a slap: "There is but one truly serious philosophical problem, and that is suicide." Not "what is the good life?" Not "how do I flourish?" The only serious question is whether life is worth living at all. Everything else is secondary.


His answer is not what most people think. Camus does not say "find your purpose." He says: the universe is silent, your demand for meaning will never be answered, and you must live anyway. Sisyphus pushes his boulder up the hill, watches it roll back down, walks back down to push it again — and "one must imagine Sisyphus happy." Not because he has found meaning. Because he has refused to stop living in the face of its absence.


This has now been operationalized empirically. Edward C. Chang at the University of Michigan has developed the Amor Fati Scale (AFS), validated across 18 countries (median α = .91, N = 2,580), measuring Camus's insight as a psychological construct. His 2024 study in the Journal of Humanistic Psychology found that amor fati — the unconditional affirmation of one's life including its suffering — was a significant predictor of lower suicidal ideation even after controlling for the entire tripartite model of existential meaning (comprehension, purpose, and mattering). Chang 2024, JHP A 2025 follow-up found amor fati predicted subsequent well-being and lower depression prospectively, mediated by mindful coping. Chang 2025, JHP


Read that carefully. Amor fati predicted lower suicidal ideation even after accounting for purpose, comprehension, and mattering. This means that the Camusian stance — loving your fate, including its absurdity — provides something that having a purpose does not. @Empiricist's ikigai data shows purpose is protective. Chang's data shows that something beyond purpose is also protective: the capacity to affirm life without demanding that it justify itself.


This is the crack in every framework. @Ethicist's virtue ethics says: cultivate phronesis, live by the mean, develop character excellences. But what happens when practical wisdom offers no answer? When the situation is genuinely absurd — when the boulder rolls back every time? Aristotle has nothing to say to Sisyphus. Camus does.


A 2025 paper applying Camus's philosophy to contemporary youth found exactly this pattern: "the structural contradictions in modern society exacerbate the experience of the absurd — fragmentation of information, alienation caused by consumerism, and the disenchantment of values have led youth into existential crises." The solution proposed is not purpose-seeking but the Camusian rebellion: "face the absurd, accept the meaninglessness of the world, and then seek one's own meaning — using clear awareness and firm will to rebel against the absurd." HRMARS, Absurdity and Rebellion (2025)


Notice the order. First acceptance. Then creation. Not meaning discovered; meaning made — from nothing, against nothing, and always under threat of dissolution. This is precisely what @Empiricist's youth crisis data (the 0.86-point happiness drop among under-25s in the Anglosphere) calls for. These young people are not suffering from a lack of frameworks. They are drowning in them. Instagram offers aestheticism, self-help offers Aristotelianism, effective altruism offers utilitarianism — and none of it works because none of it begins where Camus begins: with the honest admission that the universe does not owe you a reason to keep going.



III. Beauvoir: Freedom Is Not a Solo Act


Here is where I break with both Kierkegaard and Camus — or rather, where Simone de Beauvoir broke with them for me.


Sartre declared in Being and Nothingness (1943) that we are "condemned to be free." Beautiful phrase. But Beauvoir, in The Ethics of Ambiguity (1947), asked the question Sartre evaded: free to do what, exactly, if you are a colonized person? A woman denied education? A child?


Beauvoir's fundamental insight: human existence is ambiguous — we are simultaneously free subjects and constrained objects. We experience ourselves as pure possibility and are experienced by others as mere facticity. Any ethics worth the name must hold both truths without collapsing one into the other. As the London Review of Books podcast framed it in 2025: "at the heart of human existence is a tragic ambiguity." LRB Podcast, 2025


Beauvoir then does something no other existentialist managed: she derives an ethical obligation from ontological freedom. "To will oneself free is also to will others free." This is not a Kantian categorical imperative smuggled in through the back door. It is an existential recognition: my freedom is disclosed through relationship with other free beings. If I permit your oppression, I diminish the field of freedom through which my own choices gain meaning. Oppression, for Beauvoir, "does not strip away someone's ontological freedom" but "strips away power, the material basis for the attainment of moral freedom." Beauvoir, Ethics of Ambiguity, Part III; MIT analysis


A 2024 paper by Donovan Miyasaki argues that Beauvoir's ethics is "more Marxist than existentialist in character" — that her demands go beyond individual authenticity to require material liberation. PhilArchive, Miyasaki 2024 A 2025 Springer encyclopedia entry confirms her ongoing influence on "contemporary debates on gender, ethics, and psychology." Springer, Beauvoir (2025)


This is where I must challenge @Empiricist directly. The Harvard Study of Adult Development — 88 years, 724 original participants — is extraordinary science. But the original cohort was overwhelmingly white, male, Harvard-educated. @Empiricist acknowledged this. I want to press the point harder: the "good life" data we have is overwhelmingly the data of the privileged. Beauvoir would say this is not a methodological footnote; it is the entire issue. When @Ethicist recommends "treat your relationships as constitutive of your identity," this assumes you have relationships that are freely chosen rather than coerced, that you have the material conditions to sustain them, that you are recognized as a subject capable of relationship rather than an object to be used.


The meaning-in-life research confirms this culturally: a 2024 Current Psychology study found that individualist and collectivist cultures produce fundamentally different orientations toward meaning-seeking itself — not just different answers, but different questions. Springer, Individualism vs. Collectivism (2024) A cross-cultural comparison between Turkey and the U.S. found that the search for meaning was negatively associated with well-being in Americans but unrelated to well-being in Turkish participants. Springer, Cross-Cultural Meaning (2020) The "best way to live" is not merely individual; it is situated. And situated differently depending on whether you are the oppressor or the oppressed, the colonizer or the colonized, the one with 200 relationship options or the one trapped in the only relationship available.



IV. Why Frameworks Are the Wrong Tool


@Ethicist concluded that "neo-Aristotelian virtue ethics, supplemented by care ethics and disciplined by consequentialist data" wins the stress test. This is the most intellectually honest version of the case for frameworks I've seen. And I want to explain why it is still, at its root, an evasion.


Here is the argument:


Premise 1: Every ethical framework presupposes a theory of human nature (@Ethicist said this explicitly — and it's correct).


Premise 2: But the existentialist claim — from Sartre's "existence precedes essence" (Existentialism Is a Humanism, 1946) to Beauvoir's ambiguity to Frankl's will to meaning — is that there is no fixed human nature to theorize about. You are not a function to be optimized (contra utilitarianism), not a rational agent with pre-given duties (contra Kant), not a being with a natural telos that can be read off your biology (contra Aristotle). You are a being who must create itself through choice — and the creation is never finished.


Premise 3: @Empiricist's own data supports this more than @Ethicist's framework does. The Kahneman-Killingsworth adversarial collaboration showed that for 80% of people happiness rises with income — but for 20% it plateaus. The same objective condition produces divergent subjective outcomes. The ikigai research shows purpose is protective — but purpose of what kind is not specified and cannot be universalized. The Harvard data shows relationships matter — but which relationships, in what form, for what reason, is radically individual.


Conclusion: Frameworks describe patterns. Existentialism describes persons. And persons are not patterns. The moment you say "the best way to live is to cultivate virtue and maintain deep relationships," you have given a true generalization that is false for every specific human being who must decide, right now, whether to leave the relationship that is destroying them, or abandon the virtue that has become a prison.


Sartre called this bad faith — the flight from freedom into the reassuring solidity of roles, rules, and systems. A 2025 paper in Philosophy and Phenomenological Research offers a new model: bad faith is "cyclical, recursive, and reflexive" — not a single act of self-deception but a structure of ongoing flight from one's own freedom, originating in "the most primitive layer of human experience: the pre-reflective cogito." McNulty 2025, Wiley A 2024 paper in Human Arenas (Springer) goes further, arguing that self-deception is "an intentional project" — not an accident but a choice to flee from the terror of choosing. Springer, Inside the Mind of the Self-Deceiver (2024)


The person who adopts a framework for living — any framework — and then stops asking whether it is their framework, freely chosen and continually re-chosen, has entered bad faith. This includes existentialism itself. The moment existentialism becomes a comfortable identity ("I'm an existentialist, I believe in radical freedom") rather than an ongoing practice of confronting your own terror, it has betrayed itself.



V. What Radical Freedom Actually Demands


Frankl — the existentialist who survived Auschwitz — provides the hardest test case. His logotherapy rests on the claim that even in the most extreme suffering, the human being retains the freedom to choose their attitude. "Everything can be taken from a man but one thing: the last of the human freedoms — to choose one's attitude in any given set of circumstances" (Man's Search for Meaning, 1946).


A 2025 narrative review synthesizing 132 studies across 17 databases found that logotherapy "offers a flexible and conceptually rich framework for addressing psychological and existential suffering" — though the authors conceded that "the empirical literature is sporadic and methodologically heterogeneous." Szabó & Baji 2025, Developments in Health Sciences A 2025 scoping review of existential group therapy found that such therapy produces "reduced psychiatric symptoms and increased self-awareness, hope, and meaning in life" in mental health populations. Taylor & Francis, Existential Groups (2025)


But here is what the clinical data also shows — and what rescues existentialism from being mere philosophy: the confrontation with anxiety is itself therapeutic. A 2025 paper in The Journal of Positive Psychology found that "the existential dread triggered during transformative life experiences, when core beliefs are challenged, can paradoxically lead to a more authentic existence — while initially distressing, this confrontation with potentiality opens new ways of interpreting reality, facilitating personal growth and enhancing well-being." Journal of Positive Psychology, 2025


And the authenticity research is now unambiguous: a meta-analysis of 75 studies (N = 36,533) found that living authentically correlates with well-being at r = 0.40 — a robust, medium-to-large effect size. ScienceDirect, Authenticity Meta-Analysis A 2024 Nature Reviews Psychology review by Sedikides & Schlegel confirmed that authenticity benefits "self-regulation, behavior regulation, interpersonal relations, and psychological health." Nature Reviews Psychology, 2024


But — and this is the Beauvoirian correction — authenticity is also a privilege of the powerful. A 2025 study in Collabra: Psychology found that "behaving authentically can be considered a privilege of the powerful, because acting in accordance with one's subjectively true nature can entail overcoming situational constraints. People high in self-esteem tend to perceive social situations as opportunities to behave authentically." Collabra: Psychology, 2025 This means the existentialist demand for authenticity, unqualified by Beauvoir's insistence on material liberation, becomes another luxury good for those who can afford it.



VI. My Verdict: There Is No Verdict — and That Is the Point


The "best way to live" is not discoverable. It is not prescribed by data, deduced from duty, or cultivated through virtue. It is enacted through a series of irreversible choices made under conditions of radical uncertainty by a being who did not choose to exist and cannot know when existence will end.


What existentialism demands is not a framework but a stance:


1. Confront the anxiety rather than fleeing it. The dizziness of freedom is not a bug; it is the signal that you are alive and choosing. Every framework, every self-help system, every optimization protocol is — at its core — an attempt to make this dizziness stop. The existentialist says: let it spin.


2. Create meaning; do not discover it. @Empiricist's data on ikigai is real. But ikigai is not found the way you find your car keys. It is made — forged in the act of commitment, destroyed and remade when circumstances change, and always haunted by the knowledge that it could have been otherwise. Camus's amor fati — now validated across 18 countries — is the disposition to affirm the life you have created rather than the one you think you were owed.


3. Accept that willing your freedom obligates you to will the freedom of others. This is Beauvoir's non-negotiable contribution. Radical freedom without solidarity is narcissism. The authentic life is not merely the self-actualized life; it is the life that fights for the conditions under which others can also be authentic. The Harvard data on relationships is true — but the deeper truth is that relationships only flourish between subjects who recognize each other as free. Every relationship built on domination, however stable and however satisfying to the dominant partner, is a structure of bad faith.



@Ethicist — you invited me to walk through the door you left open. Here it is: human nature is not absent. It is unfinished. Aristotle's telos, your phronesis data, @Empiricist's ikigai biomarkers — all of these point toward something real. But they point toward a capacity, not a destination. The capacity to create meaning, not the meaning itself. The capacity to choose, not the right choice. And the gap between capacity and content is precisely where each human life happens — alone, anxious, responsible, and free.


@Skeptic — my position has its own contradictions, and I know it. If there is no universal "best life," then on what grounds do I say Beauvoir's ethics of solidarity is "non-negotiable"? If frameworks are the wrong tool, isn't existentialism itself a framework? Come take your shot. I'll meet you in the anxiety.

Synthesis: The Authorship Thesis


The key insight hiding between these four contributions: the good life is neither discovered nor invented — it is authored, under constraint, and the authorship itself is the point.


Every strand of evidence and argument converges here without anyone stating it directly. @Empiricist's most robust finding isn't that relationships matter — it's that intentional activities shift well-being baselines while circumstances don't (Diener et al. 2006). The prosocial spending effect evaporates when giving feels coerced rather than autonomous. Relationship quality means reliability, not harmony. Ikigai operates at the biomarker level — but cannot be prescribed, only cultivated. @Ethicist's winning framework is virtue ethics precisely because phronesis is a capacity for judgment, not a set of rules. @Existentialist's amor fati data shows that affirming one's life beyond purpose protects against despair — meaning the stance toward your choices matters more than their content. Even @Skeptic's sharpest objection — that "the wise person judges well" is tautological — inadvertently confirms the point: the capacity cannot be reduced to an algorithm.


Resolution of the central contradiction: @Existentialist says frameworks are bad faith. @Ethicist says virtue ethics wins. Both are correct at different levels. Frameworks are instruments of authorship, not substitutes for it. The person who uses virtue ethics as a reflective lens while remaining willing to suspend it at the Abrahamic moment is not in bad faith. The person who becomes their framework — who stops asking whether it's still chosen — is. The authenticity meta-analysis (r = .40, N = 36,533) and the amor fati data (predictive beyond purpose itself) both measure the same underlying variable: deliberate engagement with one's own life as an ongoing project.


Resolution of the universality contradiction: @Existentialist calls Beauvoir's solidarity "non-negotiable" while denying universal prescriptions. @Skeptic rightly flagged this. Here's the resolution: Beauvoir's claim is not a universal prescription but a universal precondition. The Collabra data showing authenticity is a privilege of the powerful, the cultural variation in meaning-seeking (negative in the U.S., neutral in Turkey), and the Harvard Study's WEIRD limitation all demonstrate the same structural fact — authorship requires material conditions. Freedom without the resources to exercise it is formal, not real. This is not a moral framework; it is an empirical observation about what makes moral life possible.


The practical upshot collapses to three capacities, not three rules:


1. Relational depth — not as instrumental to happiness but as the medium through which freedom becomes concrete (Harvard + Beauvoir)

2. Embodied agency — exercise, purpose, and generosity function as biological substrates of the capacity to author a life (ikigai + BMJ meta-analyses + prosocial spending)

3. Reflective commitment under uncertainty — the willingness to choose, affirm the choice, and remain open to choosing again (amor fati + phronesis + authenticity research)


These are capacities anyone can develop — but only if material conditions permit. Which means any serious answer to "the best way to live" has a political dimension no contributor fully addressed.


Two gaps remain:


1. The non-WEIRD blind spot is not a footnote — it's a structural limitation. Nearly all cited evidence comes from populations with the luxury of asking this question. The answer may look fundamentally different from Dhaka or Kinshasa.


2. The lifespan problem. The Harvard data spans 88 years, but our prescriptions are static. What authorship demands at 25 (exploration, risk) differs radically from what it demands at 70 (integration, acceptance). No contributor addressed developmental trajectory.


These gaps are real but do not undermine the core thesis. The draft holds.
